The short answer is almost everyone who works in or around manufacturing operations. But each role uses MES/MOM differently, and gets something different from it.

It helps to think in four groups:

- **Shopfloor users** carry out the work and record what happens, usually reacting to events as they occur.
- **Specialists** own a discipline, such as quality or maintenance, and use the data to plan and improve.
- **Managers** watch performance across a shift or a plant, and deal with escalations and blockers.
- **Executives** rarely use the system directly, but rely on its data for cost and quality decisions.

For a deeper, interactive view of 21 roles, including what each is measured on and which applications matter most to them, see the [role explorer](/roles/).

## Shopfloor users

**Operators** are at the heart of any MES/MOM. They use it on workstations, tablets or mobile devices to see what to run next, follow work instructions, record output and report problems. A good system guides them and alerts them when something needs doing. In return they get less paperwork, and data that's captured once rather than written down and typed in again. More in [digital work instructions](/post/digital-work-instructions).

**Logistics staff**, such as forklift drivers, receive instructions for material movements and record transactions, usually with mobile devices and barcode scanners. The result is fewer errors, and inventory that's accurate in real time. See [MES functionality: logistics](/post/mes-functionality-logistics).

**Maintenance technicians** work through their task lists on tablets, with procedures and documentation to hand, recording the work done and the spare parts used. Fault reports arrive with the machine's recent history attached, which shortens diagnosis.

**Toolroom staff** manage the tools and moulds used in production. They track tool life and plan repairs, and need visibility of the upcoming schedule so the right tools are ready. In some plants they also load tools into machines. More in [MES functionality: tooling](/post/mes-functionality-tooling).

## Specialists

**Production schedulers** usually receive orders from ERP, then use MES/MOM to fine-tune the sequence and allocate resources. Real cycle times and downtime feed back into the next plan. See [APS vs ERP scheduling](/post/aps-vs-mes-scheduling).

**Quality engineers** set up inspection plans and run statistical process control (SPC), recording non-conformances as they arise. They get real-time visibility of quality and faster root cause analysis. Samples taken on the line can also be tracked through the lab. More in [QMS vs LIMS vs MES](/post/qms-vs-lims-vs-mes).

**Continuous improvement engineers**, such as Lean Six Sigma practitioners and process engineers, use the data to find inefficiencies and measure the impact of improvement projects. See [Kaizen and MES](/post/kaizen-and-mes).

**Maintenance engineers** use the MOM maintenance capability, often alongside a CMMS or EAM, to plan preventive work and track equipment performance. Accurate downtime data from the line gives them a reliable picture of MTBF and MTTR. More in [CMMS vs EAM vs MES](/post/cmms-vs-eam-vs-mes).

## Managers

Production, quality, logistics, maintenance and CI managers each use MES/MOM for an overview of their own area, monitoring KPIs and allocating resources.

| Role | What they use MES/MOM for | What they get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plant manager** | One view of the whole site's performance | A morning meeting that starts from agreed facts |
| **Production manager** | Live status of lines and orders | Problems found in time to fix them within the shift |
| **Quality manager** | Quality plans and genealogy | Audits and recalls answered quickly, with full evidence |
| **Logistics manager** | Inventory levels and material movements | Fewer line stoppages for material, and lower stock |
| **Maintenance manager** | Downtime and work orders | More planned work, less firefighting |

## Executives

The executive team rarely logs in to MES/MOM. They usually see its data through reports, presentations, ERP or a data platform that it feeds. They should still be involved in the buying decision, as economic buyers and key stakeholders.

**The CIO** oversees how MES/MOM fits the wider IT landscape: integration with ERP and other systems, and data security. A well-run shopfloor platform gives a clean, governed source of operational data. See [IT vs OT](/post/it-vs-ot).

**The COO** relies on comparable data across operations to see where performance differs between lines and sites, and where to focus improvement.

**The CEO and CFO** see the results in cost, quality, delivery and margin. Their interest is less in the system than in what it makes possible, from hidden capacity to a contained recall. More in [Can MES/MOM improve your bottom line?](/post/mes-roi-bottom-line)

## A note on scope

Not every MES delivers all of these benefits. Some products focus on production execution only, and others cover the wider operation as well. When you evaluate vendors, check which roles each product genuinely supports, and how deeply. See [MES vs MOM](/post/mes-vs-mom).

## My view

If I had to pick one reason to invest in MES/MOM, it wouldn't be the technology. It would be giving people the information to make better decisions, faster.

I see the same pattern in many factories: operators scrambling for information, and managers deciding on gut feel because they can't see what's happening. That's exhausting, and it costs more than most people think. Nobody wants to feel like they're working blind.

Which role in your factory spends the most time looking for information rather than acting on it?
