Most AI conversations in manufacturing are about machines. Predictive maintenance, process optimisation, vision inspection.

The frontline gets less attention. I think that's a mistake, because it may be where AI makes the biggest difference to how a factory feels day to day.

## The real problem AI can help with

Imagine having your best people working all the time. On the night shift. On a bank holiday. On the first day of a new starter.

That's the opportunity. Your most experienced operators carry years of knowledge about how each machine behaves, what each product needs, and what to do when something odd happens. As they retire, that knowledge leaves with them.

AI won't replace those people. But it can help capture what they know and put it in front of everyone else, at the moment they need it.

## What works today

**An operator copilot.** A plain-English assistant on the screen the operator already uses. "What's the changeover procedure for this product?" "What does this alarm mean?" "What did we do last time this fault came up?" Answers in seconds, drawn from your procedures and history.

**Shift summaries.** Generated from what actually happened: orders, stops, quality checks, outstanding actions. The supervisor reviews instead of writing from memory. More in [Shift handover is a process](/post/digital-shift-handover).

**Real-time coaching.** When process data drifts towards a known problem, the system suggests the corrective action an experienced operator would take, before it becomes scrap.

**Visual inspection support.** Image recognition flagging likely defects for the operator to confirm, so judgement stays with the person but nothing gets missed.

## Why context decides whether it's useful

A generic chatbot can tell you what OEE is. It can't tell you why Line 4 stopped three times this morning.

To be genuinely useful on the shopfloor, an AI assistant needs to know:

- Which order is running, and on which machine
- The material, its lot and its quality status
- What's happened on this line recently: stops, reasons, results
- The current, approved procedures for this exact operation
- Who's asking, and what they're qualified to do

All of that lives in [MES/MOM](/post/what-is-mes-and-why-is-it-important-in-manufacturing). When connected worker and AI are built into the operational platform, the assistant has direct access to the context that makes its answers right. When it's a separate tool, it's guessing.

## Keep people in charge

A few principles I'd hold to:

- **Assist, don't decide.** AI suggests; the operator or supervisor decides, especially anywhere quality or safety is involved.
- **Show the source.** An answer should point back to the procedure or the data it came from, so people can check it.
- **Respect the system's rules.** If MES/MOM would block an action, an AI suggestion shouldn't get round it.
- **Learn from the floor.** Let operators flag wrong or unhelpful answers. They'll tell you quickly.

## The people who benefit most

New starters benefit most, and fastest. They get answers without interrupting someone every ten minutes, and they're guided through tasks they've only done once.

But experienced operators benefit too. Less time answering the same questions. Less time on paperwork. More time on the work that actually needs their expertise.

That's the goal. Not fewer people, but every person working closer to your best.
