Every factory has a skills matrix. Most of them live in a spreadsheet on the training manager's laptop.

It's accurate on the day it's updated. It's checked during audits. And it has absolutely no influence on who actually runs which machine at 3am on a Sunday.

## Skills management is a process

It's easy to think of skills as an HR record. It isn't. It's an operational control.

Who is qualified to run this machine? To set up this tool? To release this batch? To perform this inspection? Whose certification has expired? Who needs retraining because the procedure changed last week?

If that isn't managed, you get unqualified people running processes they shouldn't. Not because anyone is careless, but because the supervisor is short-staffed and the spreadsheet isn't in front of them.

## Recording is not enforcing

There's a big difference between a system that *records* skills and one that *enforces* them.

**Recording** tells you, after the fact, that someone without the right qualification ran the order. Useful for an audit. Too late for the product.

**Enforcing** stops it happening. The operator logs in at the machine. The system checks their qualifications against what the order and operation require. If they're not qualified, they can't start it, and the supervisor gets told why.

That's the shift from a report to a control.

## What good looks like

- **Skills defined against the work.** Not just "trained on Line 3", but qualified for specific operations, equipment and products.
- **Expiry dates that mean something.** Certifications lapse automatically and the person is flagged before it happens, not after.
- **Procedure changes trigger retraining.** When a work instruction is revised, the people qualified on the old version need to acknowledge the new one.
- **Checks at the point of work.** At login, at order start, at a critical step. Not in a monthly report.
- **A clear path to qualify.** Training tasks, sign-off by a qualified person, and a record of who signed off whom.

## Why it matters more now

The workforce problem makes this urgent.

Experienced operators are retiring. New starters arrive with less background and stay for less time. More temporary and agency workers fill gaps. Every one of those trends increases the risk that someone ends up on a task they aren't ready for.

Good skills enforcement doesn't just protect quality. It gives new starters confidence. They know the system will guide them and stop them before they make a serious mistake, and that makes them productive faster.

In regulated industries it goes further. You often have to *prove* that only qualified people performed critical steps. Doing that from a spreadsheet after the event is painful. Doing it from a system that enforced it at the time is straightforward.

## Why it belongs inside MES/MOM

A standalone skills tool can hold a beautiful matrix. But it can't stop someone starting an order, because it doesn't know an order is being started.

Enforcement needs the operational context: which order, which operation, which machine, which material. That lives in [MES/MOM](/post/what-is-mes). Put skills in the same platform and the check happens naturally, at the moment it matters.

## A simple test

Ask your supervisors one question: if an unqualified operator tried to start an order on your most critical machine tonight, what would stop them?

If the answer is "the supervisor would notice", you're relying on heroics. And heroics don't scale.
