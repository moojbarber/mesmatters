Industry 4.0. Smart manufacturing. The digital factory. The connected enterprise.

I genuinely saw "Industry 6.0" on LinkedIn recently, and it made my blood boil. We shouldn't need a version number to describe something's value. We should be able to say what it actually does.

So, labels aside, this is the definition I use.

> A smart factory is a manufacturing environment where people, processes and technology work together, supported by data, to maximise efficiency, flexibility and quality.

No magic, and no science fiction. The right information reaches the right person at the right time, and decisions are based on facts rather than gut feel.

## Why the old way is breaking

Manufacturing has always been complex. Planning, execution, quality, maintenance, inventory: none of it is new.

The old way of managing that complexity worked. Experienced people, informal communication, local decisions. The operator who tweaks the machine by ear. The supervisor who just knows. The engineer who can hear a bearing going.

It worked because the environment was stable. Fewer products, predictable demand, reliable supply, a workforce that stayed for decades.

That stability has gone. Customers want more variety, faster, with full traceability and on time every time. Costs are up across materials, energy and labour. And the experienced people who held it all together are retiring, taking the knowledge with them.

The complexity hasn't changed much. The capacity to cope with it informally has run out.

## Five things that define a smart factory

**1. Defined processes.** Clear, documented processes that make expectations, handoffs and exceptions explicit. Not tribal knowledge. Not "ask Dave, he knows how it works." Processes anyone can follow, enforced by the system, consistent across shifts, teams and sites.

**2. Planning connected to execution.** Not a schedule thrown over the wall and hoped for the best. A two-way link: the schedule reflects what's actually happening on the shopfloor, and the shopfloor sees what's coming next. When a machine goes down, the planner knows immediately.

**3. Real-time data at the point of work.** Timely, contextualised information where the work happens. Not just machine data. Work instructions, specifications, drawings and SOPs too, always the latest version. The operator shouldn't have to go looking.

**4. Decisions made from a single source of truth.** When the morning meeting starts, the data is already there. Nobody spent two hours pulling it together from spreadsheets. AI supports more of those decisions as the foundation grows, and the two develop together. Generative AI can help from day one, with procedures and know-how. AI that predicts and optimises operations learns from the data, so it gets more capable with every line you connect.

**5. People backed to make the call.** Supported by structure, not relying on heroics. When processes are clear and systems are connected, people stop firefighting and start improving.

Notice that only one of those five is really about technology. That's deliberate.

## A smart factory doesn't reduce complexity

I think this gets overlooked.

A smart factory doesn't make manufacturing simpler. The complexity is what it is. What it does is increase your capacity to handle it. Those five things create coordination capacity the informal model never had.

## What it actually delivers

Everything comes back to two things: selling more, or improving margin. Ideally both.

In practice that shows up as:

- **More from the equipment you already have.** Accurate downtime and micro-stops, visible for the first time. Customers are genuinely surprised by how much capacity was hiding.
- **Better quality.** Real-time SPC catches drift before it becomes scrap.
- **Lower inventory.** When you trust the data, you stop carrying buffers to cover for not trusting it.
- **Traceability in minutes.** A complaint that took days to investigate takes minutes.
- **Proactive maintenance.** Condition data instead of the calendar or the breakdown.
- **Faster onboarding.** New starters guided by the system rather than shadowing someone for months.

The research backs this up. In Deloitte's 2025 Smart Manufacturing Survey of 600 large manufacturers, respondents reported average improvements of 10% to 20% in production output, 7% to 20% in employee productivity and 10% to 15% in unlocked capacity ([Deloitte, 2025](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/manufacturing-industrial-products/2025-smart-manufacturing-survey.html)).

## It's a mindset, not a technology project

If you take one thing from this article, take this.

I've seen it too many times. A company buys software, expects everything to change, and six months later nothing has. The technology didn't fail. The approach did.

A smart factory means getting process, technology and people right together, and keeping them aligned:

- **Process** gives you structure: how work flows, end to end.
- **Technology** gives you scale: it supports and enforces those processes.
- **People** give you adaptability: judgement, improvement, problem-solving.
- **Data** is the foundation underneath all three.

Strengthen one and neglect the others, and you just move the imbalance somewhere else. Technology on top of undefined processes is an expensive way to automate chaos.

## Where to start

Be honest about where you are today. Asking [how long your factory takes to know what happened](/post/time-to-truth) is a good way to do that. Then pick the problems that hurt most. You have the option to start as big or small as you like, but always start with the end in mind.

Everyone wants to cross the finish line without running the race. The manufacturers who get there are the ones who start running.
