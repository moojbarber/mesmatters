Companies invest heavily in software, then wonder why adoption stalls.

They focus on features and functionality, which matter, but give the human element far less attention. But your MES/MOM is only as effective as your organisation's willingness to change.

You need good technology, and choosing the right platform matters. But even the best platform can be held back by poor processes and low adoption. You can connect every machine and integrate with ERP, and still fail.

## Every project meets resistance

In my experience, every single one does. If someone tells you their project met no resistance, they either weren't paying attention or they're not being honest.

People resist change not because they're difficult or don't care, but because change is uncomfortable. It disrupts routines and challenges expertise.

For someone who's done their job well for fifteen years, being told "we're changing how you work" can feel like being told they've been doing it wrong.

## Why good projects struggle

Even technically strong implementations can struggle when:

- Leadership didn't communicate the vision clearly
- Shopfloor teams weren't brought along on the journey
- Change management was an afterthought
- Internal expertise wasn't developed or kept

## What leadership has to do

Successful transformation is impossible without strong leadership buy-in. Leaders need to:

1. **Set the vision.** Where we're heading, and why. Communicated clearly and often.
2. **Back the change management.** Resistance is the status quo. Get this wrong and the project fails.
3. **Build a strong team.** The right people at every level, cross-functional and properly supported. More in [Building the right team](/post/mes-implementation-team).
4. **Invest in resources.** Find the knowledge and skills gaps. Upskill where you can, and hire where you can't. Put critical people in positions of ownership and accountability, and make them responsible for the outcome.
5. **Bring the detractors inside.** Find the people most likely to resist, particularly those who built the homegrown system being replaced. Give them responsibility, and incentivise them to make the new system a success.

If digital transformation is a business priority, give it the support and attention a business priority deserves.

## Bigger programmes meet less resistance

It sounds backwards, but a programme to implement MES/MOM across the whole landscape usually meets less resistance than a small one. It gets more leadership attention and more visibility, so it gets more support internally. The executives have already bought in, and the expectation is clear: people implement it and get on board.

A departmental application or a small pilot sends a different message. It looks as though the decision could still be reversed, and that's often where the most resistance comes from. It isn't a done deal, so people think they can influence it.

## Involve people early

Bring operators, supervisors, quality and maintenance into the design, not just the training.

They know the process better than anyone, including the workarounds. Their input makes the system better. And people support what they helped create.

## Explain the why

People need to understand not just how to use the new system, but why it's better for them. Less paperwork. Fewer arguments about numbers. Clearer instructions. Less time chasing information.

If the only message is "management wants more data", expect resistance. If the message is "this makes your shift easier", you'll get help.

## Invest in adoption, not just implementation

Implementation ends at go-live. Adoption starts there.

- Super users on every shift, trained early and given time to support others
- Quick wins made visible, like an operator seeing their own downtime data for the first time
- Feedback channels that actually lead to changes
- Leaders on the shopfloor, using the system themselves

Ask yourself honestly: do we have the internal capability to make this work? Have we prepared our people? Are we ready to invest in adoption, not just implementation?

Much of the difference between success and failure is the culture you build around the system.
