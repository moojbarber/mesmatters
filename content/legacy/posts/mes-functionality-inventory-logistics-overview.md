This series breaks down what MES/MOM actually does, one function at a time.

Many people think MES is just production: start the order, record counts, scrap and downtime, calculate OEE. That's the production pillar, and it's important. But operators don't stop there. They perform quality checks, consume and create material, look after their equipment, manage tools and follow procedures. A full MOM supports all of it, on the same data.

If you're new to the subject, start with [What is MES/MOM?](/post/what-is-mes-and-why-is-it-important-in-manufacturing). Then use this page to dive into each function.

## Production

**[Production monitoring](/post/mes-functionality-production-monitoring).** Running and monitoring production orders in real time: order context, machine integration, counts, scrap, downtime and the data behind OEE.

**[Production scheduling](/post/mes-basics-production-scheduling).** What ERP schedules, what MES/MOM fine-tunes, and how the shopfloor sees a live plan.

## Quality

**[Quality operations](/post/mes-functionality-quality-operations).** Inspection plans, in-line checks, SPC and control charts, non-conformances and holds, all tied back to the order and material.

## Inventory

**[Inventory management](/post/mes-functionality-inventory-management).** Raw materials, semi-finished and finished goods. Lots, containers, consumption, labelling and full traceability.

**[Warehouse management and logistics](/post/mes-functionality-logistics-warehousing).** Receiving, putaway, picking, supermarkets and line-side replenishment.

Inventory and logistics are closely tied. Inventory management is about the material itself: what it is, where it came from, what it's been used in. Logistics is about moving it to the right place at the right time.

## Maintenance

**[Maintenance](/post/mes-functionality-maintenance).** Fault reporting, work orders, planned and condition-based maintenance, and autonomous maintenance by operators, on the same downtime data production uses.

## Beyond the four pillars

**[Tooling](/post/mes-functionality-tooling).** Tool life, cavities, readiness and setups, so the order doesn't wait for the tool.

**[Energy](/post/mes-functionality-energy).** Consumption by machine, order and product, so you can see where energy actually goes.

**Connected worker.** Checklists, shift handover, skills and digital work instructions, built into the operational platform. Start with [Connected worker: one app, not five](/post/connected-worker-one-app-not-five), then read about [shift handover](/post/digital-shift-handover), [skills enforcement](/post/skills-enforcement-shopfloor) and [digital work instructions](/post/digital-work-instructions).

## Why breadth matters

Each of these functions overlaps with the others. A quality check depends on what's being produced. A material lot carries its inspection results. Downtime matters to production and maintenance alike. A tool's condition affects quality.

When they share one platform, that context is captured automatically. When they're separate systems integrated together, some of it is lost at every boundary, and operators end up switching between applications.

That's why, when you evaluate MES/MOM, it's worth asking not just "does it do production?" but "which of these functions does it cover, how deeply, and on how many data models?"
